**Learning objectives.** After this chapter you can:

1. Derive the outputs of resistor-string, R-2R and current-steering DACs and the errors that switch resistance and output impedance introduce.
2. Choose a segmentation between unary and binary cells by trading DNL, glitch and decoder cost.
3. Size unit current sources for an INL yield target with Pelgrom's law.
4. Predict the harmonic distortion caused by finite output impedance, and explain why high-speed DACs use differential outputs.
5. Compute the sinc roll-off and images of NRZ, return-to-zero and mixed-mode outputs, and the settling time a precision DAC needs.
6. Explain how dynamic element matching turns mismatch into shaped noise.

## 1. Big picture

A DAC turns a digital word into a voltage or current. It looks like the easy half of data conversion (there is no comparator and no search), but every analog impairment shows up directly in its output: a mismatched current source is a static error at every code that uses it, and a switching transient is a glitch in the waveform. The same block also hides inside most ADCs. A SAR's capacitor array (20-07), a pipeline stage's MDAC and a ΔΣ modulator's feedback DAC (20-08) all set their converter's linearity.

- **Audio** DACs in phones, earbuds and cars are oversampling ΔΣ designs with a few-bit DAC and element matching.
- **Wireless and radar transmitters** use current-steering DACs clocked at gigasamples per second that synthesize the RF signal directly.
- **Optical links** in AI datacenters drive coherent and PAM4 transmitters from DACs running at tens to over a hundred gigasamples per second.
- **Precision control and instrumentation** (test equipment, industrial loops, battery testers) use 16- to 20-bit resistor DACs with slow, accurate outputs.
- **Microcontrollers** include 8- to 12-bit string or capacitive DACs for biasing, audio prompts and waveform generation (18-06).

## 2. Intuition first

```text
 Resistor string          R-2R ladder (voltage mode)                        Current steering (one unit cell)
 VREF ──┐                  b0·VREF    b1·VREF          b(N−1)·VREF               out+ ──R_L── rail
        R   tap 2^N − 1        │          │                 │                    out− ──R_L── rail
        R   tap 2^N − 2        2R         2R                2R                    │          │
        ⋮        ⋮              │          │                 │                    D ─┤switch├─ D̄
        R   tap 1     gnd ─2R─ ● ── R ─── ● ── … ── R ───── ● ──► out              └──┬───┘
 gnd ───┘   tap 0                                                                    unit source I_u
 a switch tree connects the selected tap to the output
```

*Figure 1. Three DAC families. A string selects one of $2^N$ taps; an R-2R ladder adds binary-weighted voltages; a current-steering DAC switches unit currents between two outputs.*

**A cash-register analogy, and its limits.** To pay any amount up to \$1023 you can hold 1023 one-dollar bills (unary), or bills of 1, 2, 4, …, 512 dollars (binary), or a mix: hundred-dollar-like notes for the large part and binary small change (segmentation). Unary payments change by one bill at a time, so a slightly worn bill never makes a larger amount look smaller. With binary bills, going from \$511 to \$512 swaps every bill in your hand, and if the \$512 bill is slightly light the larger amount comes out smaller than the smaller one. That is the DNL problem of binary DACs. Unary costs many bills (area and wiring); binary costs accuracy at the big carries; segmentation buys most of the accuracy for a fraction of the cost. The analogy stops at timing: a real DAC changes its output while the bills are being swapped, and the moment when some bills have moved and others have not is a glitch that no amount of static matching removes.

## 3. Theory

> **Conventions.** $N$ bits, code $D$ from 0 to $2^N - 1$, reference $V_{REF}$ or unit current $I_u$; the ideal output is $D \cdot \text{LSB}$ with LSB $= V_{REF}/2^N$ (or $I_u$ for a unit-cell DAC). DNL and INL are endpoint-referenced, in LSB (20-06).

### 3.1 Resistor-string DACs

A string of $2^N$ equal resistors between $V_{REF}$ and ground provides every tap voltage $kV_{REF}/2^N$; a switch tree (or decoder) connects the selected tap to a buffer. Because each tap is above the one below it, the string is **inherently monotonic** whatever the resistor mismatch: DNL can never reach −1. Its weaknesses are size ($2^N$ resistors and $2^{N+1} - 2$ switches in a binary tree) and speed (the output sees the tree's resistance and the capacitance of many off switches). Strings dominate where monotonicity matters and speed does not: trim DACs, reference adjust, MCU and sensor-bias DACs (18-06). Larger designs use two strings in series (coarse and fine), keeping monotonicity with far fewer resistors.

### 3.2 R-2R ladders

An R-2R ladder needs only about $3N$ unit resistors. In the voltage-mode ladder, each bit drives a 2R leg from $V_{REF}$ (bit = 1) or ground (bit = 0) into a node; series resistors R join the nodes, and the LSB end is terminated by 2R to ground. The output is taken at the MSB node.

**Why the weights are binary.** Look left from node 0 (the LSB node): its 2R leg and the 2R termination form a Thévenin source of voltage $b_0V_{REF}/2$ and resistance R. Through the series R, that source becomes 2R, and in parallel with node 1's 2R leg it gives a source of resistance R and voltage $(b_1V_{REF} + b_0V_{REF}/2)/2$. By induction, node $k$'s Thévenin voltage is

$$
V_k = \frac{V_{REF}}{2}\left(b_k + \frac{b_{k-1}}{2} + \cdots + \frac{b_0}{2^k}\right)
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
V_{out} = V_{N-1} = \frac{V_{REF}}{2^N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}b_k2^k = \frac{D\,V_{REF}}{2^N},
$$

with output resistance R at every code. Each stage halves the contribution of everything to its left; that halving is the binary weighting.

**Switch resistance.** A switch adds $r_{sw}$ to each 2R leg but not to the termination, unbalancing the halving. The error is largest at the MSB transition: in a 10-bit ladder with $r_{sw}$ = 1% of R, the lab's nodal solution gives a worst DNL of 1.68 LSB at the 511 → 512 transition (Example 20-09.1). Designs absorb the switch into each leg (making the resistor $2R - r_{sw}$), scale switches with their legs' currents, or use current-mode ladders where the switches carry no voltage. Mismatch in the MSB legs produces the same binary-carry DNL as in any binary DAC (§3.3), so precision R-2R DACs trim or calibrate their upper bits.

### 3.3 Current-steering DACs and segmentation

A current-steering DAC switches unit current sources between two output nodes, which drive load resistors (often a doubly terminated 50 Ω line, $R_L$ = 25 Ω). No node has to settle to a new voltage inside the cell; the currents simply change direction. That makes current steering the architecture of every DAC above about 100 MS/s.

**Binary, unary and segmented.** A binary design has $N$ cells of $2^k$ units; a unary (thermometer) design has $2^N - 1$ cells of one unit, switched on one at a time as the code increases. Consider the DNL at the major carry, $2^{N-1} - 1 \to 2^{N-1}$, with independent unit errors of relative standard deviation $\sigma_u$:

- **Binary:** the MSB cell ($2^{N-1}$ units) turns on while all others ($2^{N-1} - 1$ units) turn off, so the step's error has variance $\sigma_u^2(2^{N-1} + 2^{N-1} - 1)$, and $\sigma_{DNL} = \sigma_u\sqrt{2^N - 1}$ LSB.
- **Unary:** one unit turns on: $\sigma_{DNL} = \sigma_u$.
- **Segmented** ($M$ unary MSBs, $B = N - M$ binary LSBs): the worst step turns on one unary cell of $2^B$ units and turns off $2^B - 1$ binary units: $\sigma_{DNL} = \sigma_u\sqrt{2^{B+1} - 1}$.

INL is the same for all three: it depends on the sum of the errors of the cells in use, whatever their grouping (§3.4). Segmentation therefore trades only DNL, glitch and decoder cost: unary MSBs need $2^M - 1$ cells and a thermometer decoder whose area and delay grow as $2^M$. Typical high-speed designs put 4 to 6 bits in the unary segment (Example 20-09.2).

### 3.4 Mismatch, INL and yield

With independent unit errors $\varepsilon_i$ (relative standard deviation $\sigma_u$) in a unary DAC of $N_t = 2^N - 1$ units, the endpoint INL at code $n$ is

$$
\text{INL}(n) = \sum_{i \le n}\varepsilon_i - \frac{n}{N_t}\sum_{i = 1}^{N_t}\varepsilon_i ,
\qquad
\sigma^2_{\text{INL}}(n) = \sigma_u^2\,\frac{n(N_t - n)}{N_t} ,
$$

largest at mid-scale: $\sigma_{\text{INL,mid}} = \sigma_u\sqrt{N_t}/2 \approx \sigma_u2^{N/2}/2$ LSB (the same law as 20-06's capacitor DAC).

**Yield.** For INL within ±½ LSB at mid-scale with probability $Y$, the mid-scale deviation must sit inside $C$ standard deviations, where $C$ is the two-sided normal quantile ($C$ = 3 for 99.73%): $\sigma_u \le 1/(C\sqrt{N_t})$. That estimate checks one code only. The INL path is a random walk pinned at both ends (a Brownian bridge), and its maximum over all codes is larger than its mid-scale value.

> **Going deeper — the maximum of a pinned random walk.** For large $N_t$, $\text{INL}(n)/(\sigma_u\sqrt{N_t})$ approaches a standard Brownian bridge on [0, 1], whose maximum absolute value has the Kolmogorov distribution $P(\max\lvert B\rvert \le \lambda) = 1 - 2\sum_{k\ge1}(-1)^{k-1}e^{-2k^2\lambda^2}$. Yield $Y$ for $\max\lvert\text{INL}\rvert \le 0.5$ then requires $\sigma_u \le 0.5/(\lambda_Y\sqrt{N_t})$, with $\lambda$ = 1.818 for $Y$ = 99.73%. The mid-scale estimate corresponds to $\lambda_{mid} = C/2$ = 1.5, so the exact rule asks for a $\sigma_u$ **17.5**% smaller. The lab's Monte Carlo confirms both. This Brownian-bridge treatment is published (Radulov et al., 2007 [5]). A widely quoted rule, $\sigma_u \le 1/(2C\sqrt{2^N})$ (attributed to Van den Bosch et al. [4]), compares the full-scale code with its ideal value without removing the gain error; at 99.73% it gives **0.260**% for 12 bits, a σ_u **1.65** times smaller than needed, which overdesigns the area by about 2.7×.

**Area.** Pelgrom's law (20-01, 20-11) sets the unit's mismatch. For a current source in saturation, $I \propto (V_{GS} - V_{TH})^2$, so a threshold error δ gives a relative current error $2\delta/V_{ov}$. With a single device's threshold standard deviation $A_{VT}/\sqrt{2WL}$ (the pair coefficient divided by √2) and ignoring β mismatch,

$$
\frac{\sigma_I}{I} = \frac{\sqrt2\,A_{VT}}{V_{ov}\sqrt{WL}}
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
WL = \left(\frac{\sqrt2\,A_{VT}}{V_{ov}\,\sigma_u}\right)^2 .
$$

A large overdrive reduces the area quadratically, which is why DAC current sources run at hundreds of millivolts of overdrive (at the cost of headroom). Since the allowed $\sigma_u$ scales as $1/\sqrt{N_t}$ (Pelgrom's law [11]; Razavi gives the same random-INL relation [6]), each extra bit doubles the unit area and doubles the number of units: total area grows fourfold per bit (Example 20-09.3).

### 3.5 Output impedance and distortion

A real unit cell has a finite output impedance $Z$, so a code that turns on $n$ cells presents $Z/n$ in parallel with $R_L$. With $x = R_L/Z$, the single-ended output (in units of $I_uR_L$) is

$$
v(n) = \frac{n}{1 + nx} \approx n - xn^2 + x^2n^3 - \cdots
$$

**Static INL.** Fitting the endpoint line through $v(0)$ and $v(N_t)$ leaves, to first order in $x$, $e(n) = x\,n(N_t - n)$, largest at mid-scale: $\text{INL}_{max} \approx xN_t^2/4$ LSB. Keeping it below ½ LSB requires $Z > R_LN_t^2/2$: about 210 MΩ for a 12-bit DAC into 25 Ω. Hence the cascoded unit cells of every precision current-steering DAC. Van den Bosch et al. identified this dynamic output impedance as a key SFDR limit of high-resolution DACs [4], and Luschas and Lee showed that a fully differential output needs much less of it than a single-ended one [7].

**Harmonics.** Drive the DAC with a full-scale sine, $n = (N_t/2)(1 + \sin\omega t)$. The quadratic term produces a second harmonic of amplitude $xN_t^2/8$ against a fundamental of about $N_t/2$, and the cubic term a third harmonic of $x^2N_t^3/32$:

$$
\text{HD2} \approx \frac{xN_t}{4} , \qquad \text{HD3} \approx \frac{(xN_t)^2}{16} ,
$$

the first two terms of the general result that the $n$-th harmonic is about $(xN_t/4)^{n-1}$ (Razavi's summary of Palmers and Steyaert [6]).

**Differential outputs.** The complementary output carries the other $N_t - n$ cells, $v(N_t - n)$. Subtracting the two cancels every even-order term (they are symmetric in $\sin\omega t$) and doubles the odd ones, so the differential output has HD2 = 0 and the same HD3. Since $xN_t \ll 1$, HD3 is far smaller than HD2: −72.3 dBc against −36.1 dBc in Example 20-09.4. That is why high-speed DACs are always differential.

**Why SFDR falls with frequency.** The output impedance is dominated by capacitance at high frequency, $\lvert Z\rvert \approx 1/(2\pi fC)$, so $x \propto f$: a single-ended HD2 rises 20 dB per decade and a differential HD3 40 dB per decade. Together with timing errors (§3.6), this is the dominant SFDR limit of fast current-steering DACs, and why data sheets plot SFDR against output frequency.

![Single-ended second harmonic and differential third harmonic in dBc against output frequency from 1 MHz to 2 GHz for a 12-bit DAC into 25 ohms with 1 femtofarad of code-dependent capacitance per cell: HD2 rises at 20 dB per decade from about minus 76 dBc at 1 MHz; the differential HD3 rises at 40 dB per decade from below minus 140 dBc and approaches HD2 above a gigahertz](../../../assets/figures/20-09/zout_sfdr.svg)

*Figure 3. Output-impedance distortion against frequency (the model of Example 20-09.4). Differential operation removes HD2; the remaining HD3 rises twice as fast, so the advantage shrinks as frequency rises.*

### 3.6 Dynamic errors: glitches and settling

**Glitches.** When a code changes, cells switch at slightly different times. In a binary DAC at the major carry, if the MSB cell turns on δt before the other cells turn off, the output briefly shows almost full scale. The **glitch impulse** is the area of the error, in V·s (data sheets quote pV·s or nV·s): about $(2^{N-1} - 1)\,\text{LSB} \times \delta t$. Segmentation shrinks it, because at any transition only one unary cell and the binary LSBs switch. Glitches that depend on the code produce harmonics and spurs; designs equalize switch delays, use a latch per cell to align the switching instants, and minimize the voltage swing at the switches' common node.

**Settling.** A precision DAC's output stage must settle to within ½ LSB after a full-scale step. For a single-pole response, the error after time $t$ is $e^{-t/\tau}$ of the step, so settling to $2^{-(N+1)}$ needs

$$
t_s = \tau\ln 2^{N+1} = (N + 1)\,\tau\ln 2 .
$$

A 16-bit DAC that must settle in 1 µs needs τ ≤ 84.9 ns, a bandwidth of 1.88 MHz (Example 20-09.7). Slew-rate limits add to this for large steps.

### 3.7 The output spectrum: NRZ, RZ and mixed mode

A DAC holds each sample for one period $T = 1/f_s$ (non-return-to-zero, NRZ). Its output is the impulse train of the samples convolved with a rectangular pulse of width $T$, whose Fourier transform gives the **zero-order-hold** response

$$
H_{NRZ}(f) = \frac{\sin(\pi f/f_s)}{\pi f/f_s} ,
$$

down 3.92 dB at $f_s/2$ (20 log₁₀(2/π)). The output also contains **images** of the signal at $kf_s \pm f_{out}$, weighted by the same sinc. A reconstruction filter removes them; how hard its job is depends on how close the first image ($f_s - f_{out}$) sits to the signal (Example 20-09.5).

- **Return-to-zero (RZ):** the output is held for half a period and zero for the other half. The pulse is half as wide, so the response is $\frac12\,\text{sinc}(f/2f_s)$: 6 dB less signal, but flatter (**0.91** dB of droop at $f_s/2$ instead of 3.92 dB). RZ also makes every period contain the same transitions, which reduces code-dependent glitch distortion.
- **Mixed mode (RF mode):** the output is $+x$ for the first half period and $-x$ for the second. Its response, $\text{sinc}(f/2f_s)\sin(\pi f/2f_s)$, is zero at DC and peaks in the second Nyquist zone ($f_s/2$ to $f_s$), so an RF DAC can synthesize a carrier above $f_s/2$ with more power than NRZ delivers there.

### 3.8 Dynamic element matching

Mismatch between unit elements is a fixed error for each code: a static nonlinearity. **Dynamic element matching** (DEM) chooses *which* elements represent a code differently from sample to sample, so the error becomes a signal-independent noise instead of distortion. **Data-weighted averaging** (DWA, Baird and Fiez [9]) selects the next $D$ elements in rotation after the ones used last; dynamic element matching itself goes back to Carley's 1989 noise-shaping DAC [9]. Because the pointer cycles through all elements, every element is used equally often over any stretch of samples whose codes sum to a multiple of the element count, and the mismatch error is the first difference of a bounded sequence: it is **first-order noise-shaped**, like a first-order ΔΣ loop (20-08). This is what lets multi-bit ΔΣ modulators and audio DACs use 16- or 32-element DACs with ordinary matching. DWA's weakness is tones for some inputs; randomized and higher-order variants exist.

### 3.9 Oversampling (ΔΣ) DACs

The ΔΣ idea of 20-08 runs in reverse. A digital interpolation filter raises the sample rate; a digital ΔΣ modulator truncates the word to a few bits, shaping the truncation error out of band; a few-level DAC with DEM converts it; and an analog low-pass filter removes the shaped noise. The DAC needs only a handful of elements, and their mismatch is shaped by DEM. This is the architecture of essentially every audio DAC and many precision DACs; its costs are latency (the interpolation filters) and the out-of-band noise the analog filter must remove.

## 4. Worked examples

### Example 20-09.1 · Resistor DACs (tier A)

**Setup.** A 10-bit DAC uses $V_{REF}$ = 1.2 V. Find the LSB and the output for code 683. How many resistors and tree switches does a string implementation need? In an R-2R ladder whose switches add 1% of R to every 2R leg (but not to the termination), what are the worst DNL and INL?

**Solution.**

- LSB = 1.2 V/1024 = **1.172** mV; code 683 gives $683 \times 1.2/1024$ = **0.8004** V, the same from the string or the ladder (the lab's ladder solver and ngspice agree).
- String: $2^{10}$ = **1024** resistors and $2^{11} - 2$ = **2046** switches in a binary tree.
- R-2R with 1% switch resistance: the lab's nodal solution gives a worst DNL of **1.68** LSB at the 511 → 512 transition and a worst INL of **0.84** LSB.

**Units check.** LSB in volts; DNL and INL in LSB.

**Sanity check.** The MSB leg alone sees its switch, so its weight is slightly low relative to everything to its left. That is exactly a binary-carry error: largest at mid-scale, where the MSB turns on and every other bit turns off.

### Example 20-09.2 · Segmenting a 14-bit current-steering DAC (tier B)

**Setup.** A 14-bit DAC has unit sources with σ_u = 0.5%. Compare a binary design, a fully unary design and a segmented design with 6 unary MSBs and 8 binary LSBs: cell counts, worst-case DNL standard deviation and the number of cells switching at the major carry.

**Solution.**

| Design | Cells | σ(DNL) at the worst step | Cells switching at mid-scale |
|---|---|---|---|
| Binary | **14** | $0.005\sqrt{2^{14} - 1}$ = **0.640** LSB | **14** |
| Unary | **16383** | **0.005** LSB | 1 |
| 6 + 8 segmented | $63 + 8$ = **71** | $0.005\sqrt{2^9 - 1}$ = **0.113** LSB | **9** |

**Units check.** σ(DNL) in LSB: σ_u is a relative error per unit, and each unit is one LSB.

**Sanity check.** With σ(DNL) = 0.64 LSB, a missing code (DNL < −1) is only **1.56** standard deviations away, so the binary design often has one; the segmented design keeps the risk negligible with 71 cells instead of 16,383.

### Example 20-09.3 · Sizing current sources for INL yield (tier B)

**Setup.** A 12-bit unary DAC must have |INL| ≤ ½ LSB with 99.73% yield. Find σ_u from the mid-scale estimate and from the exact maximum-INL rule, then the unit-transistor area for $A_{VT}$ = 3.5 mV·µm and $V_{ov}$ = 0.4 V, and the total active area.

**Solution.**

- Mid-scale estimate: $\sigma_u = 1/(3\sqrt{4096})$ = **0.521**%.
- Exact (Kolmogorov, λ = **1.818**): $\sigma_u = 0.5/(1.818 \times 64)$ = **0.430**%. The lab's Monte Carlo of the maximum INL confirms it: designs at the mid-scale estimate fall short of the yield target, designs at 0.430% meet it.
- Area: $WL = (\sqrt2 \times 3.5\text{ mV·µm}/(0.4 \times 0.0043))^2$ = **8.29** µm² per unit; × 4095 units = **0.034** mm².

**Units check.** mV·µm/V gives µm (scaled by 10⁻³), squared gives µm².

**Sanity check.** Each extra bit doubles the unit count and halves the allowed $\sigma_u^2$, so the total area grows fourfold per bit: a 16-bit unary design would need 256 times this area, which is why 16-bit current-steering DACs calibrate instead.

### Example 20-09.4 · Output impedance and distortion (tier B)

**Setup.** (a) A 4-bit unary DAC (15 cells of 1 mA) drives $R_L$ = 50 Ω, and each cell has $Z$ = 50 kΩ. Find the full-scale output and the worst INL. (b) A 12-bit DAC drives 25 Ω. What unit impedance keeps INL within ½ LSB single-ended? (c) At 100 MHz, suppose each cell's code-dependent impedance is that of 1 fF. Find the single-ended HD2 and the differential HD3 for a full-scale sine.

**Solution.**

(a) $x = 50/50\,000 = 10^{-3}$. Full scale: $15 \times 1\text{ mA} \times 50\text{ Ω}/(1 + 0.015)$ = **0.7389** V. INL: exact endpoint fit **0.0556** LSB; the estimate $xN_t^2/4$ gives 0.0563.

(b) $Z > R_LN_t^2/2 = 25 \times 4095^2/2$ = **210** MΩ.

(c) $\lvert Z\rvert = 1/(2\pi \times 100\text{ MHz} \times 1\text{ fF})$ = **1.59** MΩ, $x$ = 1.57 × 10⁻⁵, $xN_t$ = 0.0643. Estimates: HD2 ≈ $xN_t/4$ → −35.9 dBc; HD3 ≈ $(xN_t)^2/16$ → −71.8 dBc. Exact (the lab's harmonic analysis of $n/(1 + nx)$): single-ended HD2 **−36.1** dBc; differential HD2 cancels, HD3 **−72.3** dBc.

**Units check.** $x$ and $xN_t$ are dimensionless; harmonics in dBc.

**Sanity check.** The differential output buys **36.1** dB here. At 1 GHz, $x$ is ten times larger and the differential HD3 rises to **−36.3** dBc with these (pessimistic) numbers, 36 dB in one decade (the 40 dB rule holds only while $xN_t \ll 1$): the cell's internal capacitance is the central concern of RF DAC design.

### Example 20-09.5 · Sinc droop and images (tier A)

**Setup.** A DAC runs at $f_s$ = 6 GS/s. For a 2.4 GHz output, find the NRZ droop, the level of the first image at 3.6 GHz, and the RZ response at 2.4 GHz. What does mixed mode deliver at 3.6 GHz, in the second Nyquist zone, against NRZ?

**Solution.** $f/f_s$ = 0.4 and 0.6.

- NRZ at 2.4 GHz: $20\log_{10}[\sin(0.4\pi)/(0.4\pi)]$ = **−2.42** dB.
- First image (3.6 GHz): **−5.94** dB, only **3.52** dB below the signal.
- RZ at 2.4 GHz: $20\log_{10}[\tfrac12\,\text{sinc}(0.2)]$ = **−6.60** dB.
- At 3.6 GHz: mixed mode **−3.17** dB against NRZ's −5.94 dB, **2.77** dB more power for a carrier synthesized in the second zone.
- At $f_s/2$: NRZ **−3.92** dB, RZ **−6.93** dB.

**Units check.** Gains in dB relative to NRZ's DC gain.

**Sanity check.** RZ loses 6.02 dB at DC but only **3.01** dB more than NRZ at $f_s/2$, so its passband is flatter, as §3.7 says.

### Example 20-09.6 · Glitch impulse (tier B)

**Setup.** A 12-bit, 1 V full-scale DAC switches from code 2047 to 2048. In a binary design the MSB cell switches 50 ps before the others. In a 6 + 6 segmented design, the new unary cell switches 50 ps before the six binary cells turn off. Estimate each glitch impulse.

**Solution.**

- Binary: for 50 ps the new MSB (2048 LSB) and the old cells (2047 LSB) are all on, so the output shows 4095 instead of its final 2048: **2047** LSB too high. Impulse $2047 \times (1\text{ V}/4096) \times 50\text{ ps}$ = **25.0** pV·s.
- Segmented: the new unary cell (64 LSB) is on while the binary cells (63 LSB) are still on: 63 LSB too high. Impulse $63/4096 \times 50$ ps = **0.769** pV·s, **32.5** times smaller.

**Units check.** V × s = V·s; 1 pV·s = 10⁻¹² V·s.

**Sanity check.** The ratio is the ratio of cells that move, 2047 LSB of binary weight against 63: segmentation turns the worst glitch into a small one at every carry.

### Example 20-09.7 · Settling a precision DAC (tier A)

**Setup.** A 16-bit DAC's output buffer behaves as a single pole. What time constant lets a full-scale step settle to ½ LSB in 1 µs, and what bandwidth is that?

**Solution.** $\ln 2^{17}$ = **11.78** time constants: τ ≤ 1 µs/11.78 = **84.9** ns, a bandwidth $1/(2\pi\tau)$ = **1.88** MHz.

**Units check.** Seconds over a dimensionless count; $1/(2\pi\tau)$ in hertz.

**Sanity check.** Each bit costs $\ln 2$ = 0.69 time constants: a 4-bit DAC needs $5\ln2$, so 16 bits take **3.4** times as long for 4096 times the precision. Exponential settling makes precision cheap in time; it is the final value's accuracy that is hard.

## 8. Common mistakes

- **Expecting binary DACs to be monotonic.** At the major carries the MSB's error is compared with all other bits combined; binary DNL grows as $\sigma_u2^{N/2}$.
- **Thinking segmentation improves INL.** It does not; INL depends on the total of the unit errors in use, whatever their grouping. Segmentation fixes DNL and glitches.
- **Ignoring the termination in an R-2R ladder.** A switch resistance that is not mirrored in the termination unbalances the halving at every stage.
- **Specifying output impedance at DC only.** SFDR at gigahertz is set by the cell's capacitance, which falls 20 dB per decade.
- **Reading SFDR without the output frequency.** A DAC's SFDR at 10 MHz says little about its SFDR at 2 GHz.
- **Forgetting the sinc.** An NRZ DAC is 3.9 dB down at $f_s/2$ and its images are only a few decibels below a signal near $f_s/2$.
- **Quoting settling time for small steps.** A full-scale step may slew before it settles; check the step size behind a settling specification.

## 9. Practitioner lens

### State of the art (as of Nov 2026)

- **Segmented current steering is the high-speed standard [Established].** The classic papers set the pattern: a 10-bit 500 MS/s design in 0.6 mm² with SFDR above 60 dB to 200 MS/s (Lin and Bult, 1998), a 14-bit 150 MS/s "Q² random walk" design reaching 14-bit accuracy without trimming (Van der Plas et al., 1999) and a 10-bit 1 GS/s design with more than 61 dB SFDR to Nyquist (Van den Bosch et al., 2001) [1]–[4]. Today's RF-sampling parts keep the structure: TI's DAC39RF10 describes "Thermometer encoded current sources/switches representing the upper MSBs", a middle segment and "Binary weighted current sources/switches representing the lower LSBs" [12].
- **Linearity is now dynamic [Established].** Recent papers fight code-dependent switching rather than static mismatch: a 16-bit 10 GS/s design holds SFDR above 70 dBc to 2.86 GHz (JSSC 2026), and a 14-bit 20 GS/s RF DAC reaches about 70 dBc IMD3 up to 8.9 GHz with dynamic element matching aimed at inter-symbol interference (ISSCC 2026) [10]. Commercial parts randomize their unit cells too: the DAC39RF10's "Dynamic element Mixing" "whitens the non-linearity", at the cost that "Both DEM and dither increase the noise floor" [12].
- **The fastest DACs serve AI-datacenter optics and wireline [Established].** An 800 Gb/s coherent-optics DSP integrates four reconfigurable 60–160 GS/s 8-bit DACs with 57 GHz of analog bandwidth in 5 nm (ISSCC 2024); a 224 Gb/s PAM4/PAM6 wireline transmitter in 3 nm uses a 7-bit DAC driver split into 4 binary and 3 thermometer bits (ISSCC 2024) [10]. Their abstracts do not give power or effective bits.
- **No public performance survey [Established, as far as we could find].** Unlike ADCs (20-06's Murmann survey), DACs have no standard public survey or agreed figure of merit; papers define their own, so compare them with care [R-347].
- **Precision DACs are resistive [Established].** 20-bit R-2R parts such as the DAC11001A add an internal track-and-hold deglitcher for "code-independent ultra-low glitch (1 nV-s)", disabled for large steps [13]; 16-bit string DACs such as the DAC8568 trade INL for guaranteed monotonicity and 0.1 nV·s glitches [13].

### Why it matters commercially

DACs sit at every boundary where digital content becomes a physical signal, and the learner's coverage areas touch all of them. In **AI datacenters**, every coherent optical module and every DAC-based SerDes transmitter (the PAM4 links between accelerators and switches) is built around a tens-of-gigasample DAC inside a DSP; the DAC's resolution, bandwidth and power per lane are part of the link's power budget. In **wireless and radar**, RF-sampling DACs let one chip synthesize several bands directly at gigahertz, displacing analog mixers and filters. In **wearables, earbuds and XR**, audio DACs are oversampling ΔΣ designs whose specifications are mostly digital-filter and clock-jitter questions. In **industrial and test equipment**, 16- to 20-bit precision DACs set the accuracy of control loops. A due-diligence view should separate the analog core from the digital wrapper around it: in the DAC39RF10's highest-performance mode, about **60**% of the power is drawn on the 1.0 V digital, encoder and SerDes rail, not by the current-steering core (our arithmetic) [12].

### Questions for engineering leaders

1. **"What are SFDR, IMD3 and noise density at our carrier frequency, over the full Nyquist band, at the full-scale current we will use?"** Strong: tables across output frequency and current. Evasive: low-frequency SFDR or a windowed number.
2. **"Which spurs are excluded, and over what window?"** Strong: full-band SFDR with every spur, and windowed numbers labelled as such. Evasive: "in-band SFDR" without a definition.
3. **"Is dynamic element matching or dither on in these numbers, and what does it cost in noise floor?"** Strong: NSD with and without it. Evasive: spurs with DEM and noise without.
4. **"What is total power, including the interface and interpolation, in our operating mode?"** Strong: per-rail power for the mode. Evasive: core power.
5. **"Is INL specified over full temperature, without calibration modes, and is it production-tested?"** Strong: guaranteed limits over temperature. Evasive: a front-page number with footnotes.

### Marketing vs Reality

- **"16-bit."** The DAC39RF10 has a 16-bit core but specifies INL of ±9 LSB and DNL of ±2.2 LSB typical, and an effective resolution of 9.6 bits from noise-power-ratio measurements; its −155 dBFS/Hz noise floor alone, integrated over 1 GHz, would limit SNR to about **65** dB [12]. The DAC38RF82 is a "14-Bit resolution" DAC with a 16-bit input mode, and its typical INL and DNL were revised from ±1 and ±0.5 LSB to ±4 and ±3 LSB between data-sheet revisions in 2017 [12].
- **"94 dBc SFDR."** That is the DAC38RF82's SFDR "within 500 MHz fOUT ± 250 MHz"; over the whole first Nyquist zone the same part specifies 62 dBc at 501 MHz [12]. Ask for the window and the excluded spurs.
- **SFDR at low frequency.** The DAC39RF10 gives 85 dBc at 97 MHz and 61 dBc at about 3 GHz; Van der Plas et al.'s 14-bit 150 MS/s DAC gave 84 dB at 500 kHz and 61 dB at 5 MHz [3], [12].
- **"1-LSB INL" and "fast settling."** The DAC11001B's front-page "1-LSB INL (max)" applies over 0–70 °C, by design rather than production test, with temperature calibration enabled; over −40 to 125 °C the limit is ±2 LSB. Its "1 µs" settling is to 0.1% of full scale, about **10.0** bits; settling to ±1 LSB at 20 bits takes 3 µs [13].
- **Glitch "energy."** It is a voltage-time area, quoted for a one-LSB step at the major carry, at one output range; a string DAC's 0.1 nV·s and an R-2R DAC's 4 nV·s differ by **40** times for the same 16 bits [13].
- **Sanity check.** Convert every claim to the operating point you care about: SFDR and IMD3 at the carrier, full band, full-scale current; NSD integrated over the signal bandwidth; power for the whole mode; INL over temperature without calibration.

## 10. Concept check

**Q1.** Why is a resistor-string DAC always monotonic?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Each tap is one resistor above the previous tap, and every resistor has a positive value, so the tap voltages increase with the code whatever their mismatch: DNL is always greater than −1.

</details>

**Q2.** Why does an R-2R ladder produce binary weights?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Looking left from any node, the ladder is a Thévenin source of resistance R; through the series R it becomes 2R, which in parallel with the next node's 2R leg halves the contribution of everything to the left. Each stage halves, so the bits are weighted by powers of two, and the output resistance is R at every code.

</details>

**Q3.** What does segmentation improve, and what does it not?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

It improves DNL and glitch at the major carries, because a unary MSB cell is compared only with the binary LSBs it replaces, and fewer cells switch at once. It does not improve INL, which depends on the sum of all the unit errors in use.

</details>

**Q4.** Why do high-speed current-steering DACs use differential outputs?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Finite cell output impedance makes the single-ended output compress as $n/(1 + nx)$, a mainly quadratic error that produces a large second harmonic. The complementary output carries the opposite code, so subtracting the two cancels all even-order terms, leaving a much smaller third harmonic.

</details>

**Q5.** Why does a DAC's SFDR fall at high output frequencies?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The cells' output impedance becomes capacitive, so $x = R_L/\lvert Z\rvert$ grows in proportion to frequency and the impedance-induced harmonics rise (20 dB per decade for HD2, 40 for HD3); timing skews and code-dependent glitches also matter more as the signal changes faster.

</details>

**Q6.** What does data-weighted averaging do to element mismatch?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

It rotates through the elements so that each is used equally often over time; the mismatch error becomes the first difference of a bounded sequence, that is, noise shaped to high frequencies like a first-order ΔΣ loop, instead of a fixed nonlinearity.

</details>
